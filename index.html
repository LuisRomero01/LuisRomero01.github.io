Citizen Four - Blog Post Four

After watching Citizenfour, one of the things that surprised me most was how much Edward Snowden was willing to sacrifice to expose the NSA's surveillance programs. I had heard about Snowden before, but I never really understood the extent of what he revealed or how drastically it changed his life. I found it admirable that he was willing to give up his career, freedom, and life in the United States because he believed the public deserved to know what was happening.

Another thing that stood out to me was how much information the government was collecting on ordinary citizens. I had always assumed that some level of surveillance was happening, but I never realized just how extensive it was. It made me think about how much personal information I share through my phone, computer, and other devices without giving it much thought. It's concerning how easily we sacrifice our privacy for convenience, especially when we don't fully understand where that information ends up or who has access to it.

Finally, the documentary made me question where we should draw the line between national security and individual privacy. I understand that surveillance can be an important tool for preventing threats, but I don't think that should automatically justify collecting information on everyone. What surprised me most was that it took someone risking everything for the public to learn about these programs. Overall, Citizenfour changed the way I think about digital privacy and made me more conscious of how much trust we place in both the government and technology companies.

Hive Tracks Discussion- Blog Post Three

One of the most interesting things I learned from our discussion about HiveTracks was how much the company has evolved since its creation. I was surprised to learn how many times the platform had been rebuilt using different technologies to keep up with modern software development. It was also interesting to learn that much of the development and security work is outsourced rather than handled in-house.

Another thing that stood out to me was the discussion surrounding data ownership and privacy. Before this case study, I hadn't really considered who owns the data collected by agricultural applications. Although beekeepers are responsible for generating the data, third-party companies may still have the ability to collect, analyze, and potentially profit from it. This raises some interesting ethical questions about how much control users should have over their information.

Finally, I was surprised to learn that the legacy platform had over 40,000 registered users across more than 150 countries. I hadn't realized how widely used HiveTracks was, especially considering its focus on such a specific industry. Overall, this case study helped me better understand the technical and ethical challenges involved in maintaining a software platform.

Ethics Discussion- Blog Post Two

For our classes ethics discussion, I talked about the area I grew up in and how the diversity was able to shape who I am today and how I make decisions. One thing I forgot to mention was how being an uncle has also been a big influence in my life. Ive been an uncle since I was in the fifth grade, so majority of my life has been being told to be a role model for my nieces and nephews. This new responsibility made me think a lot about my decisions and motivated me to pursue goals that may seem hard, just so I can hopefully be a person my younger family members can look up to, same way my brother, dad and uncles did for me.

I enjoyed having the ethics discussion in class because it made me realize we are pretty much the same when it comes to deciding how to treat people, how we make decisions, and our overall morals. One specific discussion that stood out to me was the “would you go back in time to kill baby hitler”, not only because it was funny, but because one person made a good point that we already know that hitler eventually dies and World War Two comes to an end, but if we were to take out hitler, we might open the door for another person to take the same place and possibly not loose. This funny yet though provoking discussion reminded me what my mom used to tell me as a kid, “things happen for a reason, good or bad, its how you react that makes you who you are”.

Blog Post One

My best “in-class” computing experience has to be working on the projects for my AI class. I consider this one of my favorite experiences because it made me feel like an actual scientist. I was creating charts, comparing results with my peers, and adjusting models to get the best results. Although the class was a bit tedious, I still enjoyed the stress of having a neural network take forever to load because my laptop only had 8 GB of RAM at the time.

My best “outside-of-class” experience was participating in the 2026 Hackathon at App. This was probably one of the first times I truly felt like a software developer. My friends and I created a web service that made it easier for students, faculty, and visitors to find their lost items by providing a website that tracks lost items turned in to different buildings. I got to work on the backend side of the project, and I really enjoyed planning which frameworks, databases, and features we were going to use. Luckily, we ended up winning the “Most Practical” award and got some Nerds Clusters as a prize.
